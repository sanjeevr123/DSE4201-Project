\section{LITERATURE REVIEW}\label{ch:lit}

This chapter positions the thesis against the work closest to it. It follows the argument of the thesis in six steps: what regime switching is known to add to risk forecasts; how much of that gain survives a strong baseline; how correlation has been modelled and where regime-switching correlation has been evaluated; what is known about the stock--bond correlation itself; how forecasts are evaluated and compared; and how null results are best reported. It ends with what I did not find. I read some sources in full and others at the level of the abstract, and the reading level matters to what I can say, so I state it where a claim depends on it. Several sources were available to me only as working papers, and published versions may differ.

\subsection{Regime-switching volatility and risk forecasting}\label{sec:lit-ms}

Markov switching goes back to \citet{hamilton1989}, and regime shifts matter for asset allocation \citep{ang2002}, where regime-switching correlations and volatilities rise in bad times. For risk forecasts, \citet{ardia2018} report that Markov-switching GARCH models give more accurate VaR, ES and left-tail forecasts than single-regime counterparts, especially for stock returns, and that the improvement is larger when switching is added to simple specifications such as a normal GARCH. They score with the loss of \citet{patton2019}, which I also use. Their object is the volatility of a single return series. Whether letting the dependence between assets switch adds anything is a different question, and it is the one this thesis asks.

\subsection{Gains relative to the strength of the baseline}\label{sec:lit-baseline}

The size of a regime gain depends on what it is measured against. \citet{ardia2018} find the gain largest over simple baselines. In the multivariate literature, \citet{fortin2023} describe earlier studies of univariate against multivariate volatility models for portfolio VaR as giving mixed results; I take this characterisation from their text and not from the studies. What I know of those studies is their abstracts: \citet{santos2013} report that multivariate GARCH models outperform univariate ones out of sample for portfolio VaR, with dynamic conditional correlation and Student-$t$ errors the most appropriate; \citet{brooks2003} find that the relative accuracy of volatility forecasts is highly sensitive to the measure used to evaluate it; \citet{mcaleer2008} find that single-index models give excessive and serially dependent VaR violations and portfolio models too few; and \citet{kole2017} find that temporal aggregation matters most for a ten-day VaR and that differences between asset-level and aggregated modelling are smaller. In their own evaluation of weekly portfolio forecasts (the 2021 working paper), Fortin et al.\ report that static multivariate models tend to be dominated by univariate and dynamic multivariate ones, and that dynamic multivariate models are mostly not significantly different from a univariate skewed-$t$ GARCH-type model. A single-regime GARCH model is therefore a strong baseline, and the confirmatory comparison of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} gives both models the same GARCH margins, so that a gain cannot be credited to a weak benchmark.

\subsection{Correlation modelling and regime-switching correlation}\label{sec:lit-corr}

Correlation can be held constant \citep{bollerslev1990}, driven by a GARCH-type recursion \citep{engle2002}, or switched between states. \citet{pelletier2006} proposes the last: a Markov chain selects one of several correlation matrices, each constant within its regime, combined with separate models for the standard deviations. A restricted version scales all correlations by a single regime factor, and the two-step estimation separates the correlation parameters from the volatility ones. In the version I read, a working paper of May 2003, the model is applied to four exchange rates over 946 daily observations and compared with a DCC model by in-sample likelihood and by a non-nested test; the regime model fits better in sample and gives smoother correlations. I did not find an out-of-sample forecast comparison or a VaR or ES evaluation in that version. It also notes that autocorrelations of the cross-products of standardised residuals have little power to reveal dynamic correlation. My model RSC is of the same family, but it differs from his in two ways: the regime signal is a hidden Markov model fitted to SPY returns and not a chain estimated jointly with the correlations, and the confirmatory version switches a single pair of correlations and not the whole matrix.

The closest design that I have read is the working-paper version of \citet{paolella2019}: GARCH(1,1) margins that do not depend on the regime, regime-specific correlation matrices, and a forecast that mixes the regime densities with predicted regime probabilities. In 29 Dow Jones stocks they report better density forecasts from regime-switching correlation than from constant correlation. For value-at-risk they report that accuracy is largely determined by the heavy tails of the return distribution and not by the correlation dynamics, and that regime switching does not generally improve the backtest outcome. They score with log scores and coverage tests and not with a joint VaR and ES loss, and they use mainly 29 Dow Jones stocks, with some indices and exchange rates, and no stock--bond portfolio. I have not read the published version.

The evaluation that is closest in method is \citet{fortin2023}, which compares three univariate with eight multivariate copula models, several with dynamic correlation, for weekly Value-at-Risk and expected shortfall of portfolios of equity factors and of stocks. I read two working-paper versions, from 2018 and 2021; they differ in sample, tail levels and loss, and I describe the 2021 one. It uses 1,905 weekly out-of-sample forecasts for 1983--2019, tail levels of 1\% and 2.5\%, the joint loss of \citet{patton2019}, the conditional predictive ability test of \citet{giacomini2006} and the model confidence set of \citet{hansen2011}. It reports that in most cases the differences are not significant, and concludes that the differences are mostly not significant. The design leaves two things open that I take up. It does not use regime-switching correlation, and, in the versions I read, it reports average losses, $p$-values and sets without an interval for the loss difference and without a power calculation, so a non-significant result is not shown to be small. I have not read the published text.

\subsection{The sign and drivers of the stock--bond correlation}\label{sec:lit-drivers}

The stock--bond correlation has changed sign. \citet{baele2010} document realised correlations of about 60\% in the mid-1990s and about $-60\%$ in the early 2000s, and \citet{campbell2020} report that the correlation of bond and stock returns was positive on average before the second quarter of 2001 and negative afterwards. The two studies disagree about the drivers. \citet{campbell2020} link the switch to a change in the sign of the correlation between inflation and the output gap, which they date to 2001 with a break test on macroeconomic data, and show in a habit-formation model, whose macroeconomic parameters they estimate separately for the two periods, that time-varying risk premia amplify the change. \citet{baele2010} study a dynamic factor model with ten state variables, including macroeconomic, risk-aversion, uncertainty and liquidity variables, and find that macroeconomic fundamentals explain little of the stock--bond correlation, that liquidity variables matter more, and that a model with constant exposures cannot generate negative correlations; they obtain them when the exposures depend on the variance premium. They also find that a component model that uses daily returns forecasts realised correlations better than their factor model, and that the factor model fails mostly in fitting covariances. Neither study forecasts risk, and both end before 2012.

Two points follow. The correlation has changed sign over multi-year spells, which motivates letting it switch. But whether the state behind the change is a slow macroeconomic one or a faster market-based one is not settled: the evidence in one study points to the macroeconomy and in the other away from it. This bears on the regime signal. \citet{connolly2005} relate the stock--bond return correlation to stock-market uncertainty, measured by implied volatility, which fits the market-based reading; I know that study at the level of its abstract. A two-state model of SPY volatility ties the correlation regime to equity-market stress in the same way. \citet{forbes2002} show that heteroskedasticity biases correlation-based tests of contagion, a reminder that correlation estimated in volatile periods needs care. I do not test either reading.

\subsection{Forecast evaluation and tests of predictive ability}\label{sec:lit-eval}

Joint elicitability of VaR and ES \citep{fissler2016} underlies the loss of \citet{patton2019}, whose FZ0 form gives loss differences that are homogeneous of degree zero when forecasts are negative. Coverage is tested by \citet{kupiec1995}, and \citet{christoffersen1998} tests whether the indicator of a breach is independent and identically distributed with the right probability against a first-order Markov alternative. He shows that the likelihood ratios for coverage and independence add up to the test of conditional coverage when the first observation is conditioned on, and, in a simulation, that the test of independence needs large samples and has little power when the coverage of the interval is close to 0.5 or to 0.95. These tests describe one forecast at a time. Equal predictive ability is tested by \citet{diebold1995}, who allow general loss functions and correlated forecast errors, and \citet{west1996} extends inference to non-nested and nonlinear models whose parameters are estimated. \citet{giacomini2006} take the forecasting method, including its estimation window, as the object of evaluation and allow models of either kind with a finite rolling window; \citet{mccracken2020} shows that the unconditional statistic can diverge for a fixed finite estimation window, and notes that with a rolling window the theorem of Giacomini and White remains valid. This is the setting of my test. For linear models of which one is a special case of the other, under squared-error loss, \citet{clark2001} show that the limits are non-standard and that comparing the unadjusted statistic with normal critical values makes it undersized, and \citet{clark2007} propose an adjusted statistic that can be compared with normal critical values approximately. The primary pair is nested in one correlation entry and some other pairs are nested in the correlation matrix (Table~\ref{tab:differs}), but the loss is not squared error and the models are not linear, so I treat those results as a warning about size and check size in simulation (Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}) instead of relying on them. Superior predictive ability across several candidates is tested by \citet{hansen2005}.

\subsection{Negative results, power and pre-registration}\label{sec:lit-null}

\citet{hoenig2001} argue that power calculations made after the experiment to interpret a non-significant result are fundamentally flawed, which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} reports power only for stated effect sizes. \citet{altman1995} make the point that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, \citet{ioannidis2017} report that the median power across 159 economics literatures is 18\% or less, and \citet{abadie2020} argues that non-significant results can be highly informative in large-sample settings and should be reported visibly. \citet{lakens2017} proposes equivalence tests with bounds set by the smallest effect of interest, which is the idea behind the bound that I use in outcome O3, although I do not claim equivalence. \citet{nosek2018} describe pre-registration as separating prediction from postdiction. I know these six sources at the level of abstracts. Standard results that I use without a comment on reading level, such as the Diebold--Mariano statistic with its small-sample factor, Holm's procedure, the Kupiec test and the Newey--West estimator, are cited from the registry record or the abstract and were not re-read in full.

\subsection{What this thesis adds}\label{sec:lit-adds}

I did not find a study that does all of the following at once: separates the treatment of volatility, correlation and the mean under one regime signal; evaluates VaR and ES forecasts of a fixed portfolio with a joint loss and a test whose estimand, sidedness and decision rule were fixed before the test sample was used; and reports minimum detectable differences and simulated power next to every result. This is a statement about what I read, not a claim that no such study exists. The sources above leave the confirmatory question open: whether letting the stock--bond correlation switch with an equity-volatility regime improves tail-risk forecasts over constant correlation, with the same GARCH margins in both models, in a period that contains the 2022 reversal. Chapters~\ref{ch:eval} and~\ref{ch:holdout} give the design and the answer.
