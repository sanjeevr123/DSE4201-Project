\section{Literature Review}\label{ch:lit}

This chapter positions the thesis against the work closest to it, in the order of its argument: what regime switching adds to risk forecasts and how much of that gain survives a strong baseline; how correlation has been modelled and where regime-switching correlation has been evaluated; what drives the stock--bond correlation; and how forecasts are compared and null results reported.

\subsection{Regime switching in risk forecasting}

\subsubsection{Regime-switching volatility and risk forecasts}\label{sec:lit-ms}

Markov switching goes back to \citet{hamilton1989}, and regime shifts matter for asset allocation \citep{ang2002}, where regime-switching correlations and volatilities rise in bad times. For stocks and bonds together, \citet{guidolin2006} find that two or three states describe each asset's returns alone but that four states, which they label crash, slow growth, bull and recovery, are needed for the joint distribution. The regimes that matter for one asset need not be those that matter for its comovement with another. For risk forecasts, \citet{ardia2018} report that Markov-switching GARCH models give more accurate VaR, ES and left-tail forecasts than single-regime counterparts, especially for stock returns, and that the improvement is larger when switching is added to simple specifications such as a normal GARCH. They score with the loss of \citet{patton2019}, which I also use. Their object is the volatility of a single return series. Whether letting the dependence between assets switch adds anything is a different question, and it is the one this thesis asks.

\subsubsection{Gains relative to the strength of the baseline}\label{sec:lit-baseline}

The size of a regime gain depends on what it is measured against. \citet{ardia2018} find the gain largest over simple baselines. In the multivariate literature, \citet{fortin2023} find that static multivariate models tend to be dominated by univariate and dynamic multivariate ones, and that dynamic multivariate models are mostly indistinguishable from a univariate skewed-$t$ GARCH-type model (Section~\ref{sec:lit-corr} describes their design). A single-regime GARCH model is therefore a strong baseline, and the confirmatory comparison of Chapter~\ref{ch:method} gives both models the same GARCH margins, so that a gain cannot be credited to a weak benchmark.

\subsection{Stock--bond correlation: models and drivers}

\subsubsection{Correlation modelling and regime-switching correlation}\label{sec:lit-corr}

Correlation can be held constant \citep{bollerslev1990}, driven by a GARCH-type recursion \citep{engle2002}, or switched between states. \citet{pelletier2006} proposes the last: a Markov chain selects one of several correlation matrices, each constant within its regime, combined with separate models for the standard deviations, and two-step estimation separates the correlation parameters from the volatility ones. Applied to four exchange rates and compared with a DCC model by in-sample likelihood and a non-nested test, the regime model fits better and gives smoother correlations; there is no out-of-sample VaR or ES evaluation. My model RSC is of the same family, with two differences: the regime signal is a hidden Markov model fitted to SPY returns, not a chain estimated jointly with the correlations, and the confirmatory version switches a single correlation and not the whole matrix.

The closest design is that of \citet{paolella2019}. In its working-paper version (Swiss Finance Institute Research Paper 19-27) the model has GARCH(1,1) margins that do not depend on the regime, regime-specific correlation matrices, and a forecast that mixes the regime densities with predicted regime probabilities. On 29 Dow Jones stocks it gives better density forecasts than constant correlation, while VaR accuracy is reported to depend largely on the heavy tails of the return distribution rather than on the correlation dynamics. The published version uses a multivariate generalised hyperbolic distribution and concludes that Markov-switching correlation gives accurate risk forecasts and could lower capital requirements in distressed periods. Neither version scores VaR and ES with a joint loss or studies a stock--bond portfolio.

The evaluation closest in method is \citet{fortin2023}, which compares three univariate with eight multivariate copula models, several with dynamic correlation, for weekly VaR and ES of portfolios of equity factors and of stocks. The 2021 working-paper version uses 1,905 weekly out-of-sample forecasts for 1983--2019, tail levels of 1\% and 2.5\%, the joint loss of \citet{patton2019}, the conditional predictive ability test of \citet{giacomini2006} and the model confidence set of \citet{hansen2011}. Two things are left open that I take up: the study does not use regime-switching correlation, and it reports losses, $p$-values and model sets without an interval for the loss difference or a power calculation, so a non-significant result is not shown to be small.

\subsubsection{The sign and drivers of the stock--bond correlation}\label{sec:lit-drivers}

The stock--bond correlation has changed sign. \citet{baele2010} document realised correlations of about 60\% in the mid-1990s and about $-60\%$ in the early 2000s, and \citet{campbell2020} report that the correlation of bond and stock returns was positive on average before the second quarter of 2001 and negative afterwards. The two studies disagree about the drivers. \citet{campbell2020} link the switch to a change in the sign of the correlation between inflation and the output gap, dated to 2001 with a break test on macroeconomic data, and show in a habit-formation model that time-varying risk premia amplify the change. \citet{baele2010} study a dynamic factor model with ten state variables and find that macroeconomic fundamentals explain little of the stock--bond correlation, that liquidity variables matter more, and that negative correlations arise when exposures depend on the variance premium. Neither study forecasts risk, and both end before 2012. Evidence that covers the recent reversal points back towards the macroeconomy: using long US and UK histories, \citet{molenaar2024} find inflation, real rates and government creditworthiness to be important explanatory variables.

Two points follow. The correlation changes sign over multi-year spells, which motivates letting it switch. But whether the state behind the change is a slow macroeconomic one or a faster market-based one is not settled, and this bears on the choice of regime signal. \citet{connolly2005} relate the stock--bond return correlation to stock-market uncertainty, measured by implied volatility, which fits the market-based reading. A two-state model of SPY volatility ties the correlation regime to equity-market stress in the same way. \citet{forbes2002} show that heteroskedasticity biases correlation-based tests of contagion, a reminder that correlation estimated in volatile periods needs care.

\subsection{Evaluating forecasts and reporting null results}

\subsubsection{Forecast evaluation and tests of predictive ability}\label{sec:lit-eval}

Joint elicitability of VaR and ES \citep{fissler2016} underlies the loss of \citet{patton2019}, whose FZ0 form gives loss differences that are homogeneous of degree zero when forecasts are negative. \citet{nolde2017} argue that such consistent scoring functions should be used for comparative backtests of risk models, alongside traditional coverage tests, which is the combination used here. Coverage is tested by \citet{kupiec1995}, and \citet{christoffersen1998} tests whether the indicator of a breach is independent and identically distributed with the right probability against a first-order Markov alternative. Equal predictive ability is tested by \citet{diebold1995}, who allow general loss functions and correlated forecast errors; \citet{west1996} extends inference to models whose parameters are estimated, and \citet{giacomini2006} take the forecasting method, including its estimation window, as the object of evaluation, which is the setting of my test. Comparisons of nested models out of sample raise further issues \citep{clark2001,clark2007,mccracken2020}, which Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} explains how the design handles. Superior predictive ability across several candidates is tested by \citet{hansen2005}.

\subsubsection{Negative results, power and pre-registration}\label{sec:lit-null}

\citet{hoenig2001} argue that power calculated after the experiment to interpret a non-significant result is fundamentally flawed, which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} reports power only for stated effect sizes. \citet{abadie2020} argues that non-significant results can be highly informative and should be reported visibly, and \citet{ioannidis2017} report that the median power across 159 economics literatures is 18\% or less. \citet{lakens2017} proposes equivalence tests with bounds set by the smallest effect of interest, the idea behind outcome O3, and \citet{nosek2018} describe pre-registration as separating prediction from postdiction.

\subsection{What this thesis adds}\label{sec:lit-adds}

The thesis combines three elements that the studies above treat separately: a ladder of models that separates the treatment of volatility, correlation and the mean under one regime signal; VaR and ES forecasts of a fixed stock--bond--gold portfolio scored with a joint loss, with the test, its sidedness and its decision rule fixed before the test sample was used; and minimum detectable differences and simulated power reported next to every result. The confirmatory question is whether letting the stock--bond correlation switch with an equity-volatility regime improves tail-risk forecasts over constant correlation, with the same GARCH margins in both models, in a period that contains the 2022 reversal.
