\section{RESULTS: THE HOLD-OUT TEST}\label{ch:holdout}

This chapter reports the single pre-specified test on the hold-out (1,508 forecast days), with the sentence that Section~\ref{sec:decision-rule} fixed for the outcome that occurred. Secondary results follow, labelled as such; then the calibration of the two models, which is the result that matters most to a practitioner; and finally checks chosen after the result was known. The forecasts were produced once, from the code fixed by the tag \texttt{spec-freeze-v1}, and the run was clean: every covariance matrix was valid without repair, every GARCH fit converged and every DCC fit was admissible. Before any number was computed, the scorer's provenance check was amended once (Appendix~\ref{app:disclosure}).

\subsection{The pre-specified test}\label{sec:holdout-primary}

Letting the SPY--IEF correlation switch with the regime (RSC\_P) lowered the average loss against constant correlation (CCC) by 0.0013, a difference the test could not distinguish from zero (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-primary}). With a HAC standard error of 0.0013, $t=-1.00$ and the pre-declared one-sided $p$-value is 0.158, above the 5\% level (the critical value is $-1.6449$). The two-sided 95\% interval runs from $-0.0037$ to $+0.0012$, and the statistic is far from the $+1.96$ that would mark the switching model as significantly worse.

\input{generated/tab_holdout_primary}

This is outcome O2, because the one-sided lower bound, $-0.0033$, is below the smallest effect of interest ($-0.0020$), so O3 is not reached. The frozen sentence is:

\begin{quote}
The hold-out data neither establish an improvement nor exclude one. The estimate is $-0.0013$ (95\% CI $[-0.0037,+0.0012]$); an improvement of at least 0.0031 (the minimum detectable difference for the one-sided 5\% test, 80\% power) would have been detected with 80\% probability, and smaller improvements, including none, are consistent with the data.
\end{quote}

The frozen sentence needs three qualifications. First, the estimate is 0.4 times the minimum detectable difference, so the design could not detect an improvement of this size with any useful probability; the standard error, 0.0013, lies within the range of 0.0010 to 0.0015 that the development data had suggested (Section~\ref{sec:pair-dev}). Second, at the standard error observed, a true improvement equal to the smallest effect of interest, 0.0020, would have been detected with a probability of about 48\%; this figure was computed after the result was known, for that stated effect and not at the estimate. Third, the interval contains zero and also improvements well above the smallest effect of interest, up to $-0.0037$, and a degradation up to $+0.0012$. I therefore claim neither equivalence with constant correlation nor an improvement.

\subsection{Secondary comparisons}\label{sec:holdout-secondary}

These are the secondary analyses declared in Section~\ref{sec:prereg}; they are unadjusted, not confirmatory, and leave the outcome unchanged.

\input{generated/tab_holdout_secondary}

The ladder on the hold-out (first panel of Table~\ref{tab:holdout-secondary}) repeats the development pattern without adding precision. Switching all correlations gives $-0.0037$ ($p=0.265$, MDE 0.0092). The three comparisons that involve the pooled-normal and static models have estimates between $-0.095$ and $-0.130$ but standard errors more than twenty times those of the primary pair, so the hold-out says very little about them. The static step D against B is positive, $+0.0155$ ($p=0.186$), and carries the oversizing caution of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

Changing only the tail level or the chain leaves the sign unchanged: at $\alpha=2.5\%$ the estimate is $-0.0026$ ($p=0.233$), and with a Student-$t$ chain at $\alpha=5\%$ it is $-0.0012$ ($p=0.339$). For the calendar year 2022 alone, the year of the reversal, the estimate is $-0.0009$ over 251 days, with an interval from $-0.0089$ to $+0.0070$; a single year is too short to test, and the interval includes a large gain and a large loss.

The Giacomini--White conditional test asks whether the loss difference depends on the lagged 63-day SPY--IEF correlation, the economic prediction of Chapter~\ref{ch:intro}. It finds no such dependence (Wald statistic 1.40 on two degrees of freedom, $p=0.498$). In the data, 667 of 1,508 days (44.2\%) had a positive lagged correlation; the mean loss difference was $-0.0004$ on those days and $-0.0019$ on the others. These means are descriptive, and their direction runs opposite to the mechanism that motivated the question. The test of superior predictive ability against GARCH-$t$, with the candidates B, D, RSC and RSC\_P, is also non-significant (consistent $p=0.477$).

\subsection{Calibration}\label{sec:holdout-calibration}

\input{generated/tab_holdout_calib}

Both models were breached more often than intended, by about the same amount (Table~\ref{tab:holdout-calib}). At the 5\% level the VaR was breached on 6.9\% of days by RSC\_P and 6.8\% by CCC (104 and 103 days, against 75 expected), and the Kupiec test rejects correct coverage for both ($p=0.001$ and $0.002$). At 2.5\% the breach rates were 4.4\% and 4.3\% (Kupiec $p<0.001$). The independence test finds no clustering ($p=0.480$ and $0.444$), though with about a hundred breaches it has little power.

Breach-day losses were also larger than forecast. $Z_2$ is positive for both models (0.62 and 0.61) with a simulated $p$-value of 0.0002, the smallest that 5,000 draws can give. Because $Z_2$ combines the frequency of breaches with their size, the size alone is better read from the average return on breach days, about 1.17 to 1.18 times the forecast shortfall. In the development data the GARCH-margin models had been close to nominal (Table~\ref{tab:hits}), so the shortfall is specific to 2020--2025. Because both models fell short together, their near-equal losses say nothing about whether either is fit for use.

\subsection{Post hoc checks}\label{sec:holdout-posthoc}

The checks in this section were chosen after the result was known. They are descriptive, change no outcome, and were computed from the stored forecasts without refitting anything (scripts \texttt{thesis\_posthoc.py}, \texttt{thesis\_baselines.py} and \texttt{thesis\_fig\_cumulative.py}).

\emph{Where the difference arises.} Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative} plots the running sum of the daily loss differences. The total over all 1,508 days is $-1.91$. The switching model lost ground in the 2020 crash: the days from 15 February to 30 April 2020 sum to $+1.05$, and excluding that window the mean difference is $-0.0020$. It then gained steadily through 2021, which contributes $-1.56$, against $-0.10$ for 2020 as a whole and $-0.24$ for 2022, and the two models tracked each other from 2023. The average is therefore the net of a few large, opposite-signed episodes, with 2021, when the calendar-year SPY--IEF correlation was $-0.09$, contributing most of the favourable part and not 2022.

\emph{Inference.} The test used a HAC lag of 7. With no lag the standard error is 0.0012 ($t=-1.04$), with 21 lags 0.0014 ($t=-0.90$) and with 42 lags 0.0015 ($t=-0.84$). Because the loss difference is dominated by a few days, I also computed stationary-bootstrap intervals for the mean, which do not rely on the normal approximation: with expected block lengths of 5, 21 and 63 days they run from $-0.0037$ to $+0.0013$, from $-0.0039$ to $+0.0017$ and from $-0.0042$ to $+0.0017$. Every choice leaves the result non-significant, and the normal interval is about as wide as the bootstrap ones.

\emph{Baselines.} Three simpler forecasts of the portfolio return, scored with the same loss on the same days, were statistically indistinguishable from constant correlation. The mean FZ0 difference against CCC (positive means worse than CCC) was $+0.0317$ for GARCH-$t$ (HAC standard error 0.0429), $+0.1308$ for historical simulation (0.1278) and $+0.0063$ for the RiskMetrics EWMA (0.0440), and every interval contains zero. Their 5\% breach rates were 6.6\%, 5.8\% and 5.2\%, against 6.8\% and 6.9\% for CCC and RSC\_P; the Kupiec test rejects correct coverage for GARCH-$t$ ($p=0.008$) but not for historical simulation ($p=0.146$) or the EWMA ($p=0.673$). A one-parameter EWMA therefore came close to nominal coverage with a loss indistinguishable from that of the GARCH-based models.

\emph{Heavier tails.} The Student-$t$ versions of the two primary models (lower panel of Table~\ref{tab:holdout-calib}) breached the 5\% VaR more often, on 7.2\% of days, and the 2.5\% VaR on 4.2\%, while breach-day losses came closer to the forecast shortfall (ratio 1.07 to 1.08). Fatter tails did not fix the breach rate, which is consistent with forecast volatility that was too low rather than tails that were too thin.
